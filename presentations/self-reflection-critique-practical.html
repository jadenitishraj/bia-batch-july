01

Start With A Draft Nobody Would Reject

Before any model scores anything, the room has to agree the draft is weak. If the class only believes it is weak because an LLM said 3/10, we have taught the wrong lesson.

This is the text the project starts from. Read it out loud. It is grammatical, it is polite, it is on topic, and it would survive most review meetings.

data/weak_draft.txtthe input · deliberately mediocre
Hi everyone,

AI is changing the world and you should learn it as soon as possible. Our course will make you skilled in all AI tools and help you get better opportunities. You will study Generative AI and Agentic AI with experienced trainers and projects. This is a great chance to upgrade yourself.

Join now to become future ready.

Now the brief it was supposed to satisfy. The gap between these two files is the entire motivation for the session.

data/writing_brief.txtthe task · what "good" was supposed to mean
Write a short launch email for working professionals who are considering a live weekend program in Generative AI and Agentic AI Development.

Audience:
- Software engineers, data analysts, DevOps professionals, and tech managers
- They are busy, skeptical of hype, and want proof that the program is practical

Constraints:
- 140–180 words
- Warm but professional tone
- Mention live expert-led classes, hands-on projects, and career relevance
- Do not make unrealistic salary promises
- End with one clear call to action
Ask the room
"What is wrong with this answer, even though it sounds acceptable?" Collect answers before showing the brief, then again after. The second list is always sharper — that is the effect a rubric will have on the model, demonstrated on humans first.

What the class will name, and what it maps to

You will get most of these without prompting. Write them on the board, because in two slides they turn into rubric criteria and in four slides they turn into JSON fields.

What someone saysWhat it is really aboutBecomes
"It could be about any course"No concrete program detailSpecificity
"Better opportunities — says who?"An unsupported promiseEvidence quality
"It talks down to them"Wrong register for senior peopleAudience fit
"It is way too short"The brief asked for 140–180 wordsConstraint following
"Join now — join what?"No specific next actionCall to action
Say this out loud

"Every one of you just did in ten seconds what we are about to spend an hour teaching a program to do. You held the draft against a standard, named what was missing, and said what to change. That is the whole loop. The rest is making it repeatable at 3am without you."

Why does the practical hand you a weak draft instead of generating the first draft from the brief?
A fixed starting point makes the loop legible. If the first draft were generated fresh each run, the class could never tell whether the second version is better because the critique worked or because the generator got lucky. The project can still generate a draft — generate_first_draft() exists and you will see it on slide 7 — but the demo deliberately does not use it.
02

Two Files That Make Critique Possible

"Make it better" is not an instruction, it is a wish. These two files are what turn a vague opinion into a score you can threshold on and an instruction the next call can act on.

The rubric answers what are we measuring. Five named criteria, one scale. Notice that each line is something you could argue about with evidence — not "is it good".

data/rubric.mdwhat to measure
# Writing Quality Rubric

Score each draft from 1 to 10 using these criteria:

1. Audience fit — speaks to working professionals without hype.
2. Specificity — includes concrete program benefits, not vague claims.
3. Constraint following — respects word count, tone, and required elements.
4. Evidence quality — avoids unsupported promises or unrealistic outcomes.
5. Call to action — ends with one clear, practical next step.

Passing threshold for the classroom demo: 8/10.

The constitution answers a different question: how should the critic behave while it measures. A rubric without principles produces a critic that is technically correct and useless — it will happily invent a salary statistic to satisfy "evidence quality".

data/constitution.mdhow to judge · the guardrails
# Principles for the Critic

Use these principles while judging and improving the draft:

- Be accurate: do not invent guarantees, rankings, salaries, placements, or outcomes.
- Be specific: prefer concrete benefits over generic enthusiasm.
- Be audience-aware: working professionals have limited time and low tolerance for hype.
- Be concise: every sentence should move the reader toward a decision.
- Be useful: critique should produce revision instructions, not vague opinions.
The distinction
The rubric is the scorecard. The constitution is the conduct rules for the judge. Both go into the critic prompt on slide 5, in separate labelled sections, and you will see exactly where.

Why the last constitution line is the load-bearing one

Read it again: "Be useful: critique should produce revision instructions, not vague opinions." That single line is what makes the loop mechanical rather than decorative. A critique that says "the tone is off" ends the conversation. A critique that says "replace the opening line with a specific benefit" is an input to the next function call.

No rubric
"Make this better." Model guesses what better means. Different guess every run.
Rubric only
"Score against five criteria." Consistent scores, but the critic may invent facts to justify them.
Rubric + constitution
"Score against five criteria, honestly, and tell me what to change." Scoreable and actionable.
The trap to name now

A score is only ever as good as its rubric. An 8/10 does not mean the draft is true — it means the draft satisfied five criteria that a human wrote down, as judged by a model that cannot check facts. When correctness matters, the feedback has to come from something outside the model: a test suite, a validator, a retrieval step, a person.

Your critic keeps praising drafts that quietly invent statistics. The rubric already has an "Evidence quality" line. What is the most direct fix?
The rubric says what to look at; the constitution says how to behave while looking. "Do not invent guarantees, rankings, salaries, placements, or outcomes" is already the first line of this project's constitution — and on slide 7 you will see that a blocking severity overrides the score entirely, which is exactly the escalation this case needs.

Three roles, deliberately kept apart

The same model does all three jobs, but never in the same call. One call writes, one judges, one rewrites — each with its own system prompt. Mixing them produces a model that grades its own homework in the same breath as writing it, and it always passes.

Role 1
Generator
Produces a draft from the brief. Explicitly told not to critique itself in this step.
temperature 0.4
build_generator_messages()
Role 2
Critic
Scores the draft against rubric and constitution. Returns JSON only. Never rewrites.
temperature 0.0 · json_mode
build_critic_messages()
Role 3
Refiner
Rewrites the draft using the structured critique. Returns the draft only, no commentary.
temperature 0.3
build_refiner_messages()
Why temperatures differ
The critic runs at 0.0 because a judge that changes its mind between runs is not a judge. The generator and refiner run warmer because writing benefits from some variety. Same model, three different jobs, three different settings.

The dependency direction

main.py → critic_loop.py → prompts.py + schemas.py + llm_client.py. Nothing points back upward. That is why you can read the project bottom-up in one sitting, and it is also why the notebook and the CLI can share exactly the same engine with no duplication.

Why does the critic get its own function and its own system prompt instead of asking the generator to "write a draft and then critique it"?
Role separation buys you two things: less self-justification, and a debuggable system. When critique and generation live in one call you cannot tell whether a bad result came from a bad draft or a bad judgment, you cannot set different temperatures, and you cannot swap the critic for a stricter one later. It costs an extra API call. It is worth it.
04

schemas.py — Decide The Shape First

Before you ask a model for a critique, decide exactly what a critique is. This file is that decision, written as code that can reject a bad answer.

Start at the top. Three imports and one type alias — but that alias is the first design decision in the project.

schemas.pylines 1–10
"""Pydantic schemas for the self-reflection and critique practical."""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Literal

from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, field_validator


Severity = Literal["minor", "major", "blocking"]
What it doesSeverity is a closed set, not a string. The model may return exactly minor, major, or blocking. "pretty bad" is not a severity, and this line is what makes that a crash instead of a silent bug three functions later.

One issue the critic found

Each problem the critic reports is one of these. Look at the last field especially — revision_instruction is what makes this loop mechanical. It is not a description of the problem, it is an order the next model call can follow.

schemas.pylines 13–28
class CritiqueIssue(BaseModel):
    """One concrete issue found by the evaluator.

    Params:
        criterion: Rubric criterion the issue relates to.
        severity: Impact level for the issue.
        explanation: Plain-language explanation of the problem.
        revision_instruction: Specific instruction the refiner can use.
    Returns:
        A validated critique issue.
    """

    criterion: str = Field(..., min_length=2)
    severity: Severity
    explanation: str = Field(..., min_length=10)
    revision_instruction: str = Field(..., min_length=10)
What it doesThe min_length constraints are quiet quality control. A ten-character minimum makes "bad tone" fail validation — the critic is forced to write something long enough to be actionable.

The whole critique

Now the full evaluator output. Read the field names as a checklist of everything you want back from a judge: a number to threshold on, a verdict, a human summary, what to keep, what to fix, and two kinds of memory.

schemas.pylines 31–52
class CritiqueResult(BaseModel):
    """Structured evaluator output for a draft.

    Params:
        score: Overall score from 1 to 10.
        passed: Whether the draft is good enough to stop retrying.
        summary: Short overall judgment.
        strengths: What should be preserved in the next version.
        issues: Concrete problems to fix.
        reflection_memory: Reusable lesson for the next iteration.
        revised_strategy: How the refiner should approach the rewrite.
    Returns:
        A validated critique result.
    """

    score: int = Field(..., ge=1, le=10)
    passed: bool
    summary: str = Field(..., min_length=10)
    strengths: list[str] = Field(default_factory=list)
    issues: list[CritiqueIssue] = Field(default_factory=list)
    reflection_memory: str = Field(..., min_length=10)
    revised_strategy: str = Field(..., min_length=10)
Point at thisscore is bounded 1–10 by the schema, not by the prompt. If the model returns 47 because it misread the rubric, validation fails loudly here rather than sailing past your score >= 8 check and passing a broken draft.

Two validators follow, and they exist for teaching reasons as much as engineering ones.

schemas.pylines 54–64
@field_validator("strengths")
@classmethod
def limit_strengths(cls, strengths: list[str]) -> list[str]:
    """Keep output readable for classroom inspection."""
    return strengths[:4]

@field_validator("issues")
@classmethod
def limit_issues(cls, issues: list[CritiqueIssue]) -> list[CritiqueIssue]:
    """Keep the refiner focused on the highest-impact issues."""
    return issues[:5]
What it doesCaps the critique at four strengths and five issues. A critic that returns nineteen issues has not prioritised — and the refiner, given nineteen instructions, will follow about four of them anyway. Truncating in code makes that choice explicit instead of accidental.

One trip round the loop

The last model records a single iteration so the whole run can be replayed afterwards. The two properties are pure convenience for printing.

schemas.pylines 67–90
class IterationRecord(BaseModel):
    """One pass through the generate-critique-refine loop.

    Params:
        iteration: Iteration number.
        draft: Draft text evaluated during this iteration.
        critique: Structured critique for the draft.
    Returns:
        A validated loop record.
    """

    iteration: int = Field(..., ge=1)
    draft: str = Field(..., min_length=1)
    critique: CritiqueResult

    @property
    def score(self) -> int:
        """Return the score for quick trace printing."""
        return self.critique.score

    @property
    def passed(self) -> bool:
        """Return whether this iteration met the stopping threshold."""
        return self.critique.passed
Why it mattersBecause every iteration is stored as a validated object, the run can be serialised to JSON and inspected after class — which is exactly what save_demo_outputs() does on slide 8.

The two fields people skip past

Give these thirty seconds each, because they are the difference between this session and the previous one on prompting.

FieldWhat it holdsWho consumes it
reflection_memoryOne reusable lesson, compressed from this failureEvery later iteration, accumulated into a list
revised_strategyHow to approach the rewrite that comes nextThe refiner, on the very next call
The distinction worth drawing

revised_strategy is tactical and disposable — it is about this rewrite. reflection_memory is meant to survive: it is the sentence you would want the system to still know on attempt four. That is why the loop keeps a growing list of reflections but throws each strategy away after use.

The model returns valid JSON, but sets "severity": "critical". What happens?
This is the entire point of the file. Without the Literal, "critical" flows through happily — and then the blocking check on slide 7, which tests severity == "blocking", quietly never fires, and a draft with a fatal problem passes. A schema turns a silent wrong answer into a visible crash.

prompts.py — Three Jobs, Kept Apart

Pure functions that build message lists. No network, no state, no cleverness — which means you can print any prompt in this file without spending a rupee.

First, a small helper. It formats accumulated reflection memory for insertion into a prompt, and it handles the first iteration — when there are no reflections yet — by saying so explicitly rather than leaving a blank.

prompts.pylines 1–18
"""Prompt builders for generator, critic, and refiner roles."""

from __future__ import annotations

from typing import Iterable


def _join_reflections(reflections: Iterable[str] | None) -> str:
    """Format reflection memory for prompt context.

    Params:
        reflections: Prior lessons from the critique loop.
    Returns:
        A clean bullet list for prompt insertion.
    """
    if not reflections:
        return "No prior reflections yet."
    return "\n".join(f"- {item}" for item in reflections if item.strip())
Small but deliberate"No prior reflections yet." beats an empty string. A blank section in a prompt is ambiguous — the model cannot tell whether memory is empty or whether you forgot to send it.

Role one — the generator

The shortest prompt in the file, and note the second sentence of the system message: it is there to stop the model from helpfully critiquing itself, which would collapse two roles into one call.

prompts.pylines 21–41
def build_generator_messages(brief: str) -> list[dict[str, str]]:
    """Create messages for the first-draft generator.

    Params:
        brief: Writing brief supplied by the user or trainer.
    Returns:
        Chat messages for the generator model call.
    """
    return [
        {
            "role": "system",
            "content": (
                "You are a concise professional copywriter. Produce the first draft only. "
                "Do not critique your own answer in this step."
            ),
        },
        {
            "role": "user",
            "content": f"Create a draft for this brief:\n\n{brief}",
        },
    ]
What it doesBuilds a two-message list: a system message that fixes the role, and a user message carrying the brief. The demo does not normally call this — it starts from the fixed weak draft instead — but it is here so you can show the full generate → critique → refine cycle if you want to.

Role two — the critic

This is the most important prompt in the project. Its signature takes everything the judge needs: the original task, the draft, both criteria files, whatever has been learned so far, and the bar it has to clear.

And finally the assembled messages. Watch how every input arrives in its own labelled section — brief, rubric, principles, memory, threshold, draft — rather than being blended into one paragraph.

Role three — the refiner

The rewrite call. It receives the critique as JSON — not as prose — so the instructions arrive as discrete items rather than a paragraph the model can skim.

prompts.pylines 107–144
def build_refiner_messages(
    brief: str,
    draft: str,
    critique_json: str,
    constitution: str,
    reflections: Iterable[str] | None = None,
) -> list[dict[str, str]]:
    """Create messages for the revision step.

    Params:
        brief: Original task brief.
        draft: Current draft.
        critique_json: Structured critique as JSON text.
        constitution: Principles to preserve during revision.
        reflections: Prior lessons from earlier attempts.
    Returns:
        Chat messages for the refiner model call.
    """
    return [
        {
            "role": "system",
            "content": (
                "You are a refinement editor. Rewrite the draft using the critique. "
                "Return only the revised draft, with no commentary."
            ),
        },
        {
            "role": "user",
            "content": (
                f"Original brief:\n{brief}\n\n"
                f"Principles to preserve:\n{constitution}\n\n"
                f"Prior reflection memory:\n{_join_reflections(reflections)}\n\n"
                f"Current draft:\n{draft}\n\n"
                f"Structured critique:\n{critique_json}\n\n"
                "Rewrite the draft so it better satisfies the brief and rubric."
            ),
        },
    ]
What it does"Return only the revised draft, with no commentary." Without that line, models love to append "Here's what I changed and why" — which then becomes part of the draft that gets scored on the next iteration, and the critic marks it down for breaking the word count.
The pattern to name
Three roles, three system messages, one file. When you later swap in a stricter critic or a different house style, you edit one function and nothing else moves. That is the payoff for keeping prompts out of the loop code.
The refiner gets critique_json — the full structured critique — rather than a prose summary of it. Why?
Structure survives the handoff. A paragraph saying "it's vague and the call to action is weak" gives the refiner one blurry impression. Five objects, each with a criterion, a severity and a specific instruction, give it five things to check off — and give you the ability to ask afterwards which ones actually got fixed.

llm_client.py — One Place That Knows About OpenAI

The least interesting file in the project, and that is the achievement. Every mention of API keys, models, JSON mode and offline fallback lives here and nowhere else.

The header first. Six imports, and the one that matters for setup day is the last: dotenv is what lets a student put a key in a file instead of pasting it into code.

llm_client.pylines 1–14

Say this onceThe API key never appears in code. It lives in .env, which is listed in .gitignore. A key in a source file is a key in your git history forever.

Settings next. A frozen dataclass loaded from environment variables — so the model name and the offline switch are configuration, not code.

llm_client.pylines 17–48
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class LLMSettings:
    """Runtime settings for model calls.

    Params:
        api_key: OpenAI API key.
        model: Model name.
        offline_demo: Whether to use deterministic simulated responses.
    Returns:
        Immutable settings object.
    """

    api_key: str | None
    model: str
    offline_demo: bool


def load_settings() -> LLMSettings:
    """Load settings from environment variables.

    Params:
        None.
    Returns:
        LLMSettings with API and model configuration.
    """
    load_dotenv()
    offline_demo = os.getenv("BIA_OFFLINE_DEMO", "false").strip().lower() in {"1", "true", "yes"}
    return LLMSettings(
        api_key=os.getenv("OPENAI_API_KEY"),
        model=os.getenv("OPENAI_MODEL", "gpt-4o-mini"),
        offline_demo=offline_demo,
    )
What it doesload_dotenv() reads the .env file into the environment, then three values come out. frozen=True means settings cannot be mutated mid-run — no function can quietly switch models on you halfway through a loop.

Then a guard that fails early with a useful sentence. Compare this to what the OpenAI SDK says when the key is missing — this is the difference between a student debugging for ten minutes and a student fixing it in ten seconds.

llm_client.pylines 51–66
def check_api_ready(settings: LLMSettings | None = None) -> None:
    """Raise a friendly error if no API key is available.

    Params:
        settings: Optional preloaded settings.
    Returns:
        None. Raises RuntimeError when configuration is missing.
    """
    settings = settings or load_settings()
    if settings.offline_demo:
        return
    if not settings.api_key:
        raise RuntimeError(
            "OPENAI_API_KEY was not found. Copy .env.sample to .env and add your key, "
            "or set BIA_OFFLINE_DEMO=true for a deterministic offline classroom fallback."
        )
Classroom detailThe error message names the fix and the escape hatch. If the venue's network dies mid-session, BIA_OFFLINE_DEMO=true keeps the whole practical running on deterministic canned responses.

The offline fallback, in one line

There is a hundred-line function here that returns hand-written responses when offline mode is on. Do not open it in class. It is canned strings chosen to make the loop produce a realistic 6/10 then 9/10; reading it derails the room into discussing fake data. Show the signature, say what it is for, move on.

llm_client.pylines 69–78 · the remaining ~95 lines are canned demo text
def _offline_response(messages: list[dict[str, str]], json_mode: bool = False) -> str:
    """Return deterministic demo output when the API is unavailable.

    Params:
        messages: Chat messages from the caller.
        json_mode: Whether the caller expects JSON.
    Returns:
        Simulated text or JSON string.
    """
    combined = "\n".join(m.get("content", "") for m in messages).lower()
Say this"There is a fallback that fakes the model when we have no network. It exists so a broken wifi does not end the class. We are not reading it, because none of it is the lesson."

The one call the whole project makes

Every model call in this project — generator, critic, refiner — comes through this single function. Four parameters, one branch for offline, one for JSON mode.

        kwargs["response_format"] = {"type": "json_object"}

    # COST NOTE: With gpt-4o-mini and these small prompts, a full classroom demo
    # usually costs only a few cents. Token use grows with draft length and iterations.
    response = client.chat.completions.create(**kwargs)
    return response.choices[0].message.content or ""
Point at thisresponse_format = {"type": "json_object"} is the API-level guarantee that you get parseable JSON back. It is not a substitute for validation — the model can still return well-formed JSON with the wrong fields — but it removes an entire category of failure before Pydantic ever sees the text.

And the parser. Note the defensive first few lines: some models wrap JSON in a markdown fence even when told not to, so the code strips it rather than pretending it never happens.

llm_client.pylines 216–234
def parse_json_object(raw_text: str) -> dict[str, Any]:
    """Parse a JSON object from model output.

    Params:
        raw_text: Raw model response.
    Returns:
        Parsed JSON dictionary.
    """
    cleaned = raw_text.strip()
    if cleaned.startswith("```"):
        cleaned = cleaned.strip("`")
        cleaned = cleaned.replace("json\n", "", 1).replace("JSON\n", "", 1)
    try:
        value = json.loads(cleaned)
    except json.JSONDecodeError as exc:
        raise ValueError(f"Evaluator did not return valid JSON. Raw output:\n{raw_text}") from exc
    if not isinstance(value, dict):
        raise ValueError(f"Expected JSON object, received: {type(value).__name__}")
    return value
What it doesWhen parsing fails, the error includes the raw model output. That single detail is what turns "the evaluator returned invalid JSON" from a mystery into a two-second diagnosis.
Cost, since someone always asks

One critique call plus one refine call per failed iteration. With gpt-4o-mini and prompts this short, a full three-iteration classroom run lands in the region of a few cents — well under ₹50. Cost scales with draft length and iteration count, not with how impressive the loop looks.

JSON mode is enabled on the critic call. Why does the project still validate the result with Pydantic?
Two different guarantees. {"banana": true} is perfectly valid JSON and completely useless as a critique. JSON mode gets you past the parser; the schema gets you a critique with a bounded score, a legal severity, and an instruction long enough to act on. You want both, and they cost almost nothing together.

The critic

Here is the signature. Every input the judge needs is explicit — nothing is read from global state, which is why this function is easy to test and easy to reuse on a completely different task.

critic_loop.pylines 37–56
def critique_draft(
    brief: str,
    draft: str,
    rubric: str,
    constitution: str,
    reflections: Iterable[str] | None = None,
    pass_score: int = 8,
) -> CritiqueResult:
    """Evaluate a draft against a rubric and constitution.

    Params:
        brief: Original task brief.
        draft: Draft to evaluate.
        rubric: Scoring rubric.
        constitution: Principles for critique.
        reflections: Prior reflection memory.
        pass_score: Score required to pass.
    Returns:
        Structured critique result.
    """

And the body — six lines that do the actual work. Build the messages, call the model at temperature zero in JSON mode, parse, validate. Then the last three lines, which are the most important lines in the project.

critic_loop.pylines 57–72
messages = build_critic_messages(
    brief=brief,
    draft=draft,
    rubric=rubric,
    constitution=constitution,
    reflections=reflections,
    pass_score=pass_score,
)
raw = call_chat_model(messages, temperature=0.0, max_tokens=900, json_mode=True)
parsed = parse_json_object(raw)
critique = CritiqueResult.model_validate(parsed)

# The application owns the stopping rule. The model can recommend, but code enforces.
has_blocking_issue = any(issue.severity == "blocking" for issue in critique.issues)
critique.passed = critique.score >= pass_score and not has_blocking_issue
return critique
Stop here and pointThe model's own passed field is overwritten. Whatever the critic claimed about itself is discarded, and the verdict is recomputed from the score and the presence of a blocking issue. The model recommends; the code decides.
Why it matters
A model asked "did this pass?" is being asked to grade its own strictness. Sometimes it says yes at 6/10 because the draft "improved a lot". Recomputing the verdict in code makes the standard non-negotiable — and it means one blocking issue sinks a draft that scored 9.

The refiner

Shorter, and structurally the same: build messages, call the model, return text. The one line worth pausing on is the first one in the body.

critic_loop.pylines 75–101
def refine_draft(
    brief: str,
    draft: str,
    critique: CritiqueResult,
    constitution: str,
    reflections: Iterable[str] | None = None,
) -> str:
    """Rewrite a draft using structured critique.

    Params:
        brief: Original task brief.
        draft: Current draft.
        critique: Structured critique object.
        constitution: Principles to preserve during rewrite.
        reflections: Prior reflection memory.
    Returns:
        Revised draft text.
    """
    critique_json = critique.model_dump_json(indent=2)
    messages = build_refiner_messages(
        brief=brief,
        draft=draft,
        critique_json=critique_json,
        constitution=constitution,
        reflections=reflections,
    )
    return call_chat_model(messages, temperature=0.3, max_tokens=800).strip()
What it doescritique.model_dump_json(indent=2) turns the validated Pydantic object back into JSON text to embed in the refiner's prompt. The critique makes a round trip: model → JSON → validated object → JSON → model. Each hop is checked.
The symmetry to point out

Both functions are the same four moves — build messages, call the model, handle the response, return. Once the class sees that, the loop on the next slide is obvious: it is just these two functions in a for statement with a condition.

A critique comes back with score: 9 and one issue at severity: "blocking". What does critique_draft set passed to?
Two conditions, both required: score >= pass_score and no blocking issue. This is how you encode "some problems are not negotiable at any score" — an invented salary figure, a broken constraint, a safety violation. The score is a gradient; blocking is a gate.

The Loop, And Knowing When To Stop

Thirty lines that turn two functions into a system. The interesting part is not the iteration — it is the three separate things that can end it.

The signature first. Note initial_draft is optional: pass one and the loop starts there, pass None and it generates its own. The classroom demo passes the weak draft.

critic_loop.pylines 104–123
def run_refinement_loop(
    brief: str,
    initial_draft: str | None,
    rubric: str,
    constitution: str,
    pass_score: int = 8,
    max_iterations: int = 3,
) -> tuple[str, list[IterationRecord]]:
    """Run a generate-critique-refine loop until the draft passes or retries end.

    Params:
        brief: Original writing brief.
        initial_draft: Optional starting draft; if None, one is generated.
        rubric: Evaluation rubric.
        constitution: Principles for evaluation and revision.
        pass_score: Minimum accepted score.
        max_iterations: Maximum critique attempts.
    Returns:
        Final draft and list of iteration records.
    """

And the body. Read it slowly with the class — it is short enough to hold entirely in your head, and that is the point.

critic_loop.pylines 124–153
draft = initial_draft.strip() if initial_draft else generate_first_draft(brief)
trace: list[IterationRecord] = []
reflections: list[str] = []

for iteration in range(1, max_iterations + 1):
    critique = critique_draft(
        brief=brief,
        draft=draft,
        rubric=rubric,
        constitution=constitution,
        reflections=reflections,
        pass_score=pass_score,
    )
    record = IterationRecord(iteration=iteration, draft=draft, critique=critique)
    trace.append(record)

    reflections.append(critique.reflection_memory)

    if critique.passed:
        break

    draft = refine_draft(
        brief=brief,
        draft=draft,
        critique=critique,
        constitution=constitution,
        reflections=reflections,
    )

return draft, trace
Trace it aloudCritique the current draft → record it → append the reflection to a growing list → if it passed, stop → otherwise rewrite and go again. The reflections list is the memory: iteration three sees what iterations one and two learned.

Three ways this loop ends

Ask the class to find them before you list them. Two are visible in the code above; the third is the one people miss.

1
The draft passes
critique.passed is true — the score cleared the threshold and nothing was blocking. The break fires and the current draft is returned. This is the happy path.
2
Retries run out
The for statement finishes all max_iterations passes without a break. The loop returns the last draft it produced — which was never critiqued. Worth saying out loud: on a failing run, the returned draft has one more revision than the trace has scores.
3
A blocking issue, at any score
Set inside critique_draft on the previous slide, not here. Even a 10/10 draft cannot pass with one blocking issue — the gate is separate from the gradient.
The subtle bit worth teaching

Look at the order: the loop critiques, then refines. So the final draft on a failing run is one revision newer than the last score in the trace. If you want the returned draft to always carry a score, you critique once more after the loop — a deliberate design choice this project does not make, and a good five-minute discussion.

Printing and saving the trace

The last two functions are housekeeping, but they are what makes the run inspectable after class. The trace printer is what students will actually watch on screen.

def print_iteration_trace(trace: list[IterationRecord]) -> None:
    """Print a compact trace of scores and reflection memory.

        trace: Iteration records from the loop.

        None.
    """

        print(f"Summary: {record.critique.summary}")

def save_demo_outputs(final_draft: str, trace: list[IterationRecord], output_dir: str | Path = "demo_outputs") -> None:
    """Save final draft and trace for trainer review.

    Params:
        final_draft: Final generated draft.
        trace: Iteration records.
        output_dir: Directory to write outputs.
    Returns:
        None.
    """
    output_path = Path(output_dir)
    output_path.mkdir(exist_ok=True)

    (output_path / "final_draft.txt").write_text(final_draft, encoding="utf-8")
    trace_payload = [record.model_dump(mode="json") for record in trace]
    (output_path / "iteration_trace.json").write_text(json.dumps(trace_payload, indent=2), encoding="utf-8")
What it doesWrites final_draft.txt and iteration_trace.json into demo_outputs/. Because every record is a Pydantic model, model_dump(mode="json") serialises the whole run with no hand-written conversion code.
Three dials, one sentence
pass_score sets how good is good enough. max_iterations caps what you are willing to spend. severity == "blocking" defines what is unacceptable at any price. Every self-correcting system you build later will have these same three.
Why does the loop append critique.reflection_memory to a list before checking whether the draft passed?
The lesson from a successful attempt is still a lesson. "Specific, credible benefits outperform generic enthusiasm for this audience" is worth keeping whether or not that iteration passed — and if you later persist reflections across runs rather than discarding them, the passing ones are the most valuable of all.

Teach the plateau — do not apologise for it

When the room sees passed=False three times, you have a live demonstration of four things that are hard to argue in the abstract.

1
The code owned the stopping rule, and it said no
Every iteration the model was fairly pleased with itself. The threshold did not care. This is what "the model recommends, the code decides" looks like when it actually bites.
2
Self-critique has a ceiling
Iterations two and three scored identically and produced nearly identical reflection memory. The critic has run out of things it can notice using only its own judgment. More retries will not fix that — a different feedback source would.

A rubric can outrun the refiner
"Specificity" needs facts about the program that are in nobody's context — not the brief's, not the model's. The loop keeps asking for detail that does not exist anywhere in the inputs. That is a brief problem, not a model problem.

The line to land

What To Take Away

Five sentences worth more than the code. If the room remembers only this slide, the session worked.

1
"Improve this" is not an instruction
A rubric names what you are measuring. A constitution names how the judge should behave. Without both, critique is just a differently-worded opinion — and it changes every time you run it.
2
The model recommends. The code decides.
The critic returns a passed field and the loop throws it away, recomputing the verdict from the score and the blocking check. Every self-correcting system you build needs this line somewhere.
3
Structure is what makes feedback usable
JSON mode gets you parseable text. A schema gets you a bounded score, a legal severity, and an instruction long enough to act on. The refiner can follow five discrete items; it cannot follow a mood.
4
Reflection memory is a compressed lesson, not a transcript
One reusable sentence that changes the next attempt. If you cannot answer "what will the next iteration do differently?", the memory is decoration and you are paying tokens for it.
5
Self-critique has a ceiling, and you saw it
Two iterations at 7/10 with the same reflection. When the only feedback is the model's own opinion, the loop converges on what the model already believed. Real progress needs a signal from outside — a test, a validator, a retrieval step, a person.
And the discipline that goes with it

Do not put a critique loop on everything. It multiplies latency and cost by the number of iterations. Use it where quality genuinely justifies the spend — and where you have something better than the model's own opinion to judge against.
